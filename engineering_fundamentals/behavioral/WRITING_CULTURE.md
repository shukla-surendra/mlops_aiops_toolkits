# The Writing-Culture Company — Behavioral Interview Prep

Read `FRAMEWORK.md` first for the STAR(L) structure and how to build your story bank —
this doc is just the mapping and mechanics for a company whose internal culture is
unusually writing-heavy.

This kind of company is often unusually explicit that its values are
aspirational/directional rather than a rigid checklist, and the exact published list can
evolve over time — treat the wording below as illustrative of the recurring themes, and
verify current wording with your recruiter rather than reciting this table verbatim in an
interview.

## How the round is actually run

This company's internal culture is unusually writing-heavy — decisions are made and
defended in written docs, not meetings — and this shows up in the interview loop
directly: many senior IC, EM, and PM loops include a **written component** (a design doc,
a memo, or a take-home writing sample), evaluated alongside or sometimes instead of a pure
verbal behavioral round. Even in a purely verbal round, interviewers are implicitly
grading the *structure* of your argument, not just its content, because that's the skill
the writing culture depends on day to day.

## Values (condensed)

| Theme | What it's really asking |
|---|---|
| Move with urgency | A time you shipped a smaller, faster version deliberately instead of waiting for the complete solution. |
| Think rigorously | A time you were skeptical of an easy answer and dug into the actual mechanism before deciding. |
| Trust and amplify | A time you delegated real ownership to someone else, or made a teammate's work more visible/successful than your own. |
| Extreme ownership | A time you fixed something with no clear owner because leaving it broken wasn't acceptable to you. |
| Build for the long term | A time you paid a short-term cost (time, scope) for a decision that only paid off later. |
| High craft / user trust | Given a payments/fintech-style domain: a time you caught a subtle correctness, security, or trust issue before it became a customer-facing incident. |

## Writing sample prep

If your loop includes a written component, practice writing a one-page memo **before**
the interview, not during it — pick one of your STAR stories and rewrite the
Action/Result as a structured argument:

1. **Context** — one paragraph, enough for a reader with no prior knowledge.
2. **The decision and its rationale** — what you chose, and why.
3. **Tradeoffs explicitly considered and rejected** — this is the part most candidates
   skip. This kind of internal writing culture specifically rewards **stating the
   counterargument to your own decision and why you rejected it**; most candidates only
   narrate the path taken, not the paths considered and discarded. Doing the latter in
   writing is a specific, learnable differentiator — practice it deliberately rather than
   assuming it'll show up naturally under interview pressure.
4. **Outcome** — quantified, same as the Result in STAR.

## Company-specific failure mode

Rambling structure — in speech *or* in writing. Given the writing-heavy internal culture,
an answer that doesn't clearly separate context, decision, tradeoffs considered, and
outcome gets graded down for structure independently of whether the underlying story is
actually good. A well-chosen story told in a disorganized order will score worse here than
at a company where delivery matters less relative to content.

## Prep checklist specific to this loop

- Convert at least 2 of your story-bank entries into the 4-part written memo structure
  above, even if you don't know in advance whether your loop includes a writing exercise.
- For your "Extreme ownership" story, make sure it's genuinely about fixing something
  *nobody else owned* — not just doing your assigned job well, which is a different (and
  weaker) signal here.
- Practice stating your own decision's strongest counterargument out loud before you
  state why you rejected it — this habit is the single most distinctive skill to rehearse
  for this company, verbally or in writing.
